[Supply and demand] describes price changes based on the balance between the desire to purchase (demand) and the availability (supply) of a good. It simplifies complex market interactions into a basic equation (P = Q/I), assuming rational actors acting in self-interest. The model assumes all participants have complete information and can process it quickly, making clear predictions about price and quantity.

[Behavioral economics] complicates this picture by introducing psychological and social factors that influence individual and collective economic decisions. People often act irrationally, influenced by emotions, biases, and social norms, which can deviate from the rational actor model. For example, people might overvalue what they currently possess (status quo bias), fear loss more than desire gain (loss aversion), or be swayed by social norms (conformity). These deviations from rationality mean that simple supply and demand curves might not accurately predict market behaviors, leading to market inefficiencies and policy interventions. The model breaks down when it fails to account for these human factors, resulting in predictions that don't align with actual market outcomes.
